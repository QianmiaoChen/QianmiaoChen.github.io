Working Papers

  • Rigging the Scores: Procedural Corruption in Public Procurement Auctions
    Paper
    Abstract

    Public procurement is a cornerstone of development, yet notoriously vulnerable to corruption. This paper documents a pervasive form of procedural corruption thriving within China’s open auctions, where officials manipulate scoring rules to favor predetermined firms. To quantify the prevalence of this practice, model-driven statistical methods are developed. Applied to a vast unique dataset, the analysis reveals that an estimated 64% of procurement auctions show signs of corruption. This finding is validated by a dedicated expert audit survey, which shows an 82% agreement rate with the tool’s classifications. The analysis reveals a system of misallocation, channeling contracts to less productive, state-owned, and local firms. While broad anti-corruption campaigns prove to have limited long-term impact, counterfactual simulations from an estimated structural model show that standardizing scoring rules could increase social welfare by 4% by restoring genuine competition.

  • When Winning Isn’t Random: Detecting Favoritism in Public Procurement
    SSRN
    Abstract

    Scoring auctions give procurement officials room to favor particular firms. If competition is fair, which bidder narrowly wins a close auction should be as good as random. This paper turns that idea into a screen for favoritism, using a regression discontinuity design around the final-score cutoff in Chinese public procurement auctions. Narrow winners show systematic advantages that fair competition cannot explain. I validate the screen against bidder complaint records and the timing of anti-corruption investigations.

  • Public Procurement, Firm Networks, and the Propagation of Government Demand
    with Ming Li, Wei Lin, and [first name] Wang
    Abstract

    How does government demand spread through the economy? Linking procurement records from Guangdong, China, to firm-level VAT invoice and tax data, we trace what happens to winning firms and their suppliers after a contract award. Local winners mostly shift existing purchases toward suppliers in the procurement city, while small non-local winners genuinely expand. Larger demand shocks lead firms to reorganize their supplier networks. We use these results to ask when geographic preference clauses in public procurement are worth their cost.

  • High-Speed Railway and Local Firm Dynamics: Evidence from China
    Abstract

    The upgrade of inter-regional transport from railways to high-speed railways can significantly reduce travel time. This paper exploits the construction of China’s high-speed railway network and detailed firm registration data to study how the network shapes local economic structure and dynamics through firm entry and exit across sectors.
